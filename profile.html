The stakes of 4D scheduling — as I see them

1. Grounding AI in field reality

A schedule generated from idealized data produces an idealized plan. The hard problem is encoding real constraints — crew availability, material lead times, inspections, permits, weather windows — into structures an AI can reason about. Field experience is what turns into product specification here.

2. Risk prediction owners can act on

Predicting a delay is only useful if you know which delays matter: critical-path impacts versus absorbable float, and the difference between a two-day slip and a cascade. Judgment is what separates signal from noise in risk prediction.

3. Adoption by the people who run the work

Project managers and superintendents won't use a tool that doesn't speak their language — WBS, activities, responsibilities, drawings. A 4D product only works if it maps to artifacts the field already trusts, not the other way around.

4. Protecting margins

Delays are money. A scheduling product earns its place when it prevents the delay — by predicting risk early enough to act — not when it documents it afterwards. That is the standard I would hold every feature to.
